# A History of Piracy in the Caribbean

*As of 29 October 2026*

## Overview

Caribbean piracy ran for about three centuries, from French corsairs in the 1520s to the last pirate hangings in the 1830s. Its peak, the "Golden Age", is usually dated 1650 to 1730, and its most famous burst lasted barely a decade, from 1715 to 1726.

The whole story is driven by one fact: Spain claimed the Americas and shipped its silver home across the Caribbean, and every rival power wanted a share. Piracy rose whenever European states paid raiders to attack Spain, and fell whenever those states decided raiders were bad for their own trade.

Historians usually split the story into four phases:

| Phase | Years | Who | Main bases |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Corsairs and privateers | c.1520–1630 | French, English and Dutch raiders licensed (or tolerated) by their crowns | No fixed bases; raids from Europe |
| Buccaneers | c.1630–1697 | Anglo-French hunters turned raiders, attacking only Spain | Tortuga, Port Royal (Jamaica), Petit-Goâve |
| Golden Age pirates | c.1715–1726 | Unemployed sailors and ex-privateers attacking every nation | Nassau (New Providence, Bahamas) |
| Late piracy | c.1815–1835 | Privateers of the new Latin American republics, Cuban and Puerto Rican raiders | Barataria, Galveston, Cuban coast |

The Golden Age everyone pictures was the shortest phase, barely 11 years long.

The terms matter. A **privateer** held a government licence (a letter of marque) to attack enemy ships in wartime. A **buccaneer** was a Caribbean raider of the 1600s who attacked Spain, often with a dubious licence from a friendly governor. A **pirate** attacked anyone, with no licence at all. The same man could be all three in one career.

## Origins: Spanish silver and the first raiders (c.1520–1630)

Caribbean piracy began as a war on Spain's monopoly. The Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) split the newly found lands between Spain and Portugal, and Spain treated the whole Caribbean as a closed sea. France, England and the Netherlands never accepted this.

**The prize.** From the 1540s, silver from Potosí (Peru) and Zacatecas (Mexico) flowed through ports like Cartagena, Nombre de Dios (later Portobelo) and Veracruz. Losses to raiders forced Spain to organise convoys: the *flota* system. Two fleets sailed each year, the New Spain fleet to Veracruz and the Tierra Firme galleons to the Isthmus of Panama. They met at Havana, then crossed the Atlantic together with warship escorts.

**"No peace beyond the line."** A 1559 understanding between France and Spain held that European treaties did not apply west of the Azores. In practice, the Caribbean stayed at war even when Europe was at peace. That idea shaped raiding for a century.

**The French corsairs (1520s–1560s).** French raiders struck first and hardest:

- 1523: Jean Fleury captured ships carrying Aztec treasure that Cortés had sent to Spain.
- 1550s: François Le Clerc ("Jambe de Bois", Peg Leg) raided Cuba and Hispaniola.
- 1555: Jacques de Sores captured and burned Havana.

**The English sea dogs (1560s–1600s).** John Hawkins tried to force illegal slave-trading into Spanish ports and was ambushed at San Juan de Ulúa in 1568. His cousin Francis Drake turned to open raiding: he seized a Spanish silver mule train near Nombre de Dios in 1573, and during the Anglo-Spanish War (1585–1604) sacked Santo Domingo and Cartagena. He died of dysentery off Portobelo in 1596. Elizabeth I backed these voyages as cheap warfare, and shared the profits.

**The Dutch (1620s–1630s).** The Dutch West India Company, founded 1621, ran privateering as a business. In 1628 Piet Heyn captured an entire Spanish treasure fleet at Matanzas Bay, Cuba, the only time anyone did. The haul funded the Dutch war effort for months.

When the Anglo-Spanish War ended in 1604, unemployed English privateers turned pirate in large numbers, a pattern that would repeat after every major war.

## The buccaneers (c.1630–1697)

The buccaneers were hunters who became an unofficial navy against Spain, then outgrew their sponsors. Their peak came in the 1660s, when Henry Morgan led armies of up to 2,000 men against Spanish cities.

**Hunters on Hispaniola.** From the 1620s, French, English and Dutch drifters camped on the empty western half of Hispaniola. They hunted feral cattle and pigs and smoked the meat on a wooden frame called a *boucan*, a word borrowed from the Taíno. The French called them *boucaniers*. They sold meat and hides to passing ships, and were famously good shots with long muskets.

**Pushed to sea.** Spain saw them as trespassers and sent troops to kill them and their herds. Around 1630 many moved to Tortuga, a small rocky island off Hispaniola's north coast that was easy to defend. From there they began attacking Spanish ships in the Windward Passage between Cuba and Hispaniola, using small boats and canoes. French governors at Tortuga, and later Petit-Goâve, gave them commissions because they were a cheap defence force.

**Port Royal (1655–1692).** England seized Jamaica from Spain in 1655 under Cromwell's "Western Design". With almost no navy in the region, Jamaica's governors invited buccaneers of every nation to base at Port Royal and attack Spain. Letters of marque made them technically privateers. Port Royal became one of the richest towns in English America; by 1680 it had over 100 taverns, and a visiting clergyman called it the Sodom of the New World.

**The great raids.** Buccaneers stopped just hitting ships and started taking cities:

| Year | Leader | Target | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1666–67 | François L'Olonnais | Maracaibo and Gibraltar (Venezuela) | Sacked; 600 men in 8 ships. Notorious for torturing prisoners |
| 1668 | Henry Morgan | Puerto Príncipe (Cuba) | Taken, but poor loot; French crews left |
| 1668 | Henry Morgan | Portobelo (Panama) | Taken by land assault; about 250,000 pesos in loot and ransom |
| 1669 | Henry Morgan | Maracaibo (Venezuela) | Escaped a Spanish blockade using a fireship disguised as a warship; about 250,000 pesos |
| 1671 | Henry Morgan | Panama City | Crossed the isthmus with about 1,400 men; city burned; disappointing loot per man |
| 1683 | Van Hoorn, de Graaf, de Grammont | Veracruz (Mexico) | Sacked and held for ransom |
| 1697 | French force under de Pointis, with buccaneers | Cartagena | Sacked; last great buccaneer raid |

**Morgan's arc.** Morgan's Panama raid came after England and Spain had signed the Treaty of Madrid (1670), in which Spain finally accepted England's Caribbean colonies. Morgan was arrested and shipped to London in 1672. Instead of punishment, Charles II knighted him in 1674 and sent him back as deputy governor of Jamaica, where he now hunted the men he once led. He died a wealthy sugar planter in 1688.

**Brethren of the Coast.** Buccaneers ran themselves by agreement. Before a voyage, crews signed a *chasse-partie*, a contract setting how loot would be split and how much a man would get for losing an eye or a limb. Exquemelin, a surgeon who sailed with them, published *The Buccaneers of America* in 1678. It is the main first-hand source for the era, though it exaggerates the gore.

**The end of the buccaneers.** Three things finished them. Jamaica passed strict anti-piracy laws from the 1680s, driving buccaneers out to the Carolinas, New York and the Bahamas. An earthquake on 7 June 1692 sank about two-thirds of Port Royal into the harbour. And the Treaty of Ryswick (1697) ended war between France and Spain; France stopped using its *flibustiers*. Some buccaneers settled as planters; others went looking for richer targets in the Indian Ocean (the "Pirate Round" of the 1690s).

## The Golden Age (c.1715–1726)

The famous pirates (Blackbeard, Bellamy, Roberts, Rackham, Bonny and Read) all belong to one decade. Historian Marcus Rediker estimates about 4,000 pirates were active between 1716 and 1726, capturing roughly 2,400 ships. Unlike the buccaneers, they attacked every nation, including their own.

**Why it exploded.** Four causes came together:

1. **Peace put sailors out of work.** The Treaty of Utrecht (1713) ended the War of the Spanish Succession. Britain cut its navy from nearly 50,000 men to about 13,500, and privateering licences were cancelled. Thousands of armed, trained seamen were suddenly unemployed.
2. **Merchant service was brutal.** Low pay, bad food, and captains who flogged at will made piracy look like freedom.
3. **Trade was booming.** Sugar, slaves, tobacco and manufactured goods filled more ships than ever, with few escorts.
4. **A wreck full of silver.** On 31 July 1715 a hurricane wrecked the Spanish treasure fleet on the Florida coast. Spain set up a salvage camp; Henry Jennings raided it in early 1716. Hundreds of treasure hunters poured in, and many stayed on as pirates.

**Nassau, the "Republic of Pirates" (c.1713–1718).** Nassau, on New Providence in the Bahamas, had been wrecked by French and Spanish raids and had no working government. It had a shallow harbour that warships could not enter, a lookout hill, fresh water, and was close to the Florida Strait shipping lane. Pirates led by Benjamin Hornigold and Henry Jennings made it their base. It was never a formal republic, but an informal community of crews, traders and fences. The pirates who sailed from it are known as the Flying Gang; Rediker found that about 90% of pirates active in 1716–1726 could be traced back to two linked networks of crews, most rooted in Nassau.

**The major captains:**

| Captain | Active | Main deeds | Fate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Benjamin Hornigold | 1713–1718 | Founding figure at Nassau; mentored Teach. Reportedly refused to attack British ships | Took the 1718 pardon, became a pirate hunter; died in a shipwreck c.1719 |
| Henry Jennings | 1715–1718 | Raided the 1715 wreck salvage camp | Took the pardon; retired a planter |
| Edward Teach (Blackbeard) | 1716–1718 | Took the French slaver *La Concorde* in 1717, renamed her *Queen Anne's Revenge*; blockaded Charleston for a week in May 1718 and ransomed hostages for a chest of medicine | Killed at Ocracoke, North Carolina, on 22 November 1718 by Lt Robert Maynard |
| Samuel Bellamy | 1716–1717 | Captured the slave ship *Whydah* full of gold and silver | Drowned when the *Whydah* wrecked off Cape Cod in April 1717; wreck found 1984 |
| Stede Bonnet | 1717–1718 | Wealthy Barbados planter who bought his own ship; sailed for a time with Blackbeard | Hanged at Charleston, December 1718 |
| Charles Vane | 1716–1720 | Refused the pardon; escaped Nassau's blockade using a fireship in July 1718 | Deposed by his crew for refusing to fight a French warship; hanged at Port Royal, 1721 |
| John "Calico Jack" Rackham | 1718–1720 | Replaced Vane by crew vote; sailed with Anne Bonny and Mary Read | Captured October 1720, hanged in Jamaica; body gibbeted |
| Anne Bonny and Mary Read | 1719–1720 | Served as full crew members under Rackham | Both convicted, escaped hanging by pleading pregnancy; Read died in jail in 1721, Bonny's fate unknown |
| Bartholomew Roberts ("Black Bart") | 1719–1722 | The most successful of all; credited with hundreds of captures from Newfoundland to West Africa; flagship *Royal Fortune* carried 42 guns | Killed by HMS *Swallow* off Cape Lopez (Gabon), February 1722; 52 of his crew hanged |
| Edward Low | 1721–1724 | Known for extreme cruelty to prisoners | Disappeared; fate unknown |
| William Fly | 1726 | Mutinied and took a ship off West Africa | Hanged in Boston, July 1726, often taken as the end of the era |

Pirate numbers rose and fell fast. Rediker estimates 1,800–2,400 active pirates in 1716–1718, 1,500–2,000 in 1719–1722, and a fall to under 200 by 1726.

## How pirate ships worked

A pirate ship was run more like a small, armed cooperative than a navy ship. The crew elected and could sack its captain, a written contract set the rules, and loot was split by fixed shares. Economist Peter Leeson argues this was not idealism but good business: men who distrusted each other needed rules to cooperate.

**The articles.** Each crew drew up "articles of agreement" when it formed or elected a new captain, and every man signed or made his mark. Joining a crew was called "going on the account". Nine complete or near-complete sets survive, mostly in Charles Johnson's *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724). Bartholomew Roberts' are the best known. Typical rules:

- An equal vote on all important matters, and an equal share of food and drink.
- Fixed shares of loot (see below).
- Death or marooning for stealing from the crew or deserting in battle.
- No gambling for money on board; lights out at 8 pm; weapons kept clean and ready.
- Fighting between crew settled ashore, with pistols or swords, not on board.
- No women or boys on board (often broken, as Bonny and Read show).
- No talk of breaking up the company until each man had a set sum (Roberts' crew set £1,000).

**Who held power.**

| Role | How chosen | Power |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Captain | Elected by the whole crew; could be voted out at any time | Absolute command only in a chase or fight; otherwise one vote like anyone |
| Quartermaster | Elected; a trusted senior seaman | Spoke for the crew; kept and divided loot, handed out food, settled disputes and gave punishments; led boarding parties. A check on the captain |
| Council | The whole crew | Decided where to sail, which ships to attack, and whether to sack the captain |
| Specialists (gunner, boatswain, carpenter, surgeon) | Often taken from captured ships, sometimes by force | Paid extra shares. Surgeons often could not vote because many were forced men |

Captains were sacked often. Leeson notes one voyage where 13 captains were removed in turn. Charles Vane was voted out in 1718 for refusing to attack a French warship; Rackham replaced him on the spot. When Howell Davis was killed in 1719, the crew elected Bartholomew Roberts, who had been aboard only six weeks.

**Pay: the share system.** Under Roberts' articles the captain took 2 shares, the master, boatswain and gunner 1½, other officers 1¼, and ordinary crew 1. That is a far flatter pay gap than on merchant ships, where a captain might earn five to ten times a seaman's wage.

**Injury insurance.** Articles paid set sums from a common fund for wounds, a form of workers' compensation. Exquemelin's buccaneer contract of the 1660s paid, in pieces of eight:

| Injury | Payment (pieces of eight) |
| --- | --- |
| Loss of right arm | 600 |
| Loss of left arm | 500 |
| Loss of right leg | 500 |
| Loss of left leg | 400 |
| Loss of an eye or a finger | 100 |

Roberts' articles paid 800 pieces of eight for losing a limb.

**Who the pirates were.** Most were ordinary seamen in their twenties, average age about 25, from merchant ships, the navy or privateers. Roughly half came from Britain and Ireland, a quarter from the colonies. Black sailors, many of them escaped or freed slaves, made up a large share of many crews, though some captured Africans were sold rather than freed. Many recruits volunteered from captured ships; forcing men to join became more common in the 1720s as volunteers dried up.

**Ships.** Pirates did not build ships; they stole them. Small, fast vessels were the norm. Of pirate attacks recorded off North America in 1710–1730, David Cordingly found 55% were made in sloops, 25% in larger ships, 10% in brigs and brigantines, and the rest in schooners, open boats and snows. Sloops were shallow enough to hide among reefs and creeks, and quick to careen (clean the hull on a beach). Successful crews "traded up": Blackbeard's *Queen Anne's Revenge* was a converted slave ship with about 40 guns. Captured ships were refitted by cutting away cabins and raised decks to fit more guns and men. Pirate ships carried up to four times the crew of a merchant ship the same size, which gave them overwhelming numbers for boarding.

**Tactics: win without fighting.** Combat damaged the prize and killed crew, so pirates relied on fear. They approached under false colours, then raised a black flag: surrender now and you will be spared. A red flag meant no quarter. Reputations for cruelty to crews that resisted were deliberately spread, and most merchant crews, unpaid and outnumbered, gave up without a shot. Pirates often asked captured sailors how their captain treated them, and punished cruel captains.

## Economics: plunder, fences and colonial complicity

Piracy only worked because people on land bought what pirates stole. Merchants, officials and even governors in the colonies treated pirates as a supply line for cheap goods and hard cash, until pirates began hurting their own trade.

**What pirates actually took.** Chests of gold were rare. The everyday haul was cargo and ship supplies:

| Kind of loot | Examples | What pirates did with it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coin and bullion | Spanish pieces of eight, gold dust from West Africa | Shared out by the quartermaster; the most prized loot |
| Plantation goods | Sugar, molasses, rum, tobacco, cocoa, indigo | Sold to fences at a discount, or drunk |
| Manufactured goods | Cloth, clothing, tools, guns, gunpowder | Kept or sold |
| Ship stores | Sails, rope, anchors, cannon, timber, food, water | Used to repair and supply their own ship; often the main reason for an attack |
| Ships | Faster or larger vessels | Taken and refitted as the new flagship |
| People | Skilled sailors, surgeons, carpenters; enslaved Africans | Forced to serve, recruited, or sold |
| Information | Ship papers, letters, routes | Used to find the next target |

Goods that could not be split, like a ship or a jewel, were sold or auctioned and the money divided.

**Fences and friendly ports.** Pirates needed a market. In the 1690s, New York under Governor Benjamin Fletcher openly welcomed Red Sea pirates such as Thomas Tew. Newport, Charleston and Philadelphia merchants traded with pirates too. In the 1710s, Nassau itself was the market, and traders from the mainland colonies sailed in to buy loot cheaply. In 1718, Governor Charles Eden of North Carolina pardoned Blackbeard, and his secretary Tobias Knight was later accused of hiding pirate goods.

**Why colonists bought.** England's Navigation Acts forced colonies to buy and sell through English ports and English ships, which made imports expensive. Pirate goods skipped those rules and came cheap. Pirates also brought coin into colonies that were always short of cash. Historian Eric Jay Dolin notes that colonial attitudes only hardened once pirates started robbing local merchants in the early 1700s.

**The plantation economy turned the tide.** As sugar and slave-trading colonies grew richer, uncertainty at sea became a real cost. Pirates attacked slave ships and forts on the West African coast, and Bartholomew Roberts captured about a dozen slave ships at Ouidah in January 1722. The slave trade and sugar planters were powerful lobbies in London, and they pushed hard for suppression. Pirates were tolerated as long as they robbed Spain; once they robbed the empire's own trade, the empire crushed them.

**Privateering as a business.** Legal privateering was a regulated industry. A privateer had to bring prizes before an Admiralty prize court, which checked the capture was legal and took taxes and fees. Governors earned a cut. Buccaneers often preferred raiding towns over ships for a simple reason: loot from ships had to be shared with the authorities, but loot from towns often did not.

## Suppression and decline (1717–1730)

Britain ended the Golden Age in under ten years with a carrot-and-stick campaign: pardons first, then new laws, warships and mass hangings. By 1726 the big pirate crews were gone, and by the 1730s Atlantic piracy was rare.

**1. The carrot: the King's Pardon.** On 5 September 1717 George I issued the *Proclamation for Suppressing of Pirates*. Any pirate who surrendered to a colonial governor by 5 September 1718 would be pardoned for past crimes. A later proclamation extended the deadline. Many took it, including Hornigold and Jennings; some, like Blackbeard, took it and went back to piracy within months.

**2. Retaking Nassau.** Woodes Rogers, a former privateer famous for sailing round the world, was made governor of the Bahamas. He arrived in late July 1718 with a small fleet of warships. Most of Nassau's pirates took the pardon. Charles Vane refused, sent a burning ship at Rogers' fleet, and escaped. Rogers then hired pardoned pirates, Hornigold among them, to hunt the holdouts. In December 1718 he tried ten men for returning to piracy, convicted nine, pardoned one, and hanged eight in front of the town. A plot to restore pirate rule failed for lack of support.

**3. New laws.** The 1700 Act for the More Effectual Suppression of Piracy let colonies try pirates in local Admiralty courts, with no jury, instead of shipping them to London. The Piracy Act of 1721 went further: anyone who traded with or helped pirates could hang, and sailors who refused to defend their ship could lose their wages and be jailed for six months. This cut pirates off from both their markets and their recruits.

**4. The stick: warships and pirate hunters.** After 1713 the Royal Navy had ships to spare. Station ships patrolled the colonies, and governors hired their own pirate hunters:

| Date | Action | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sept 1718 | Col. William Rhett, hired by South Carolina, traps Stede Bonnet in the Cape Fear River | Bonnet hanged at Charleston, December 1718 |
| 22 Nov 1718 | Lt Robert Maynard, sent by Virginia's governor, attacks Blackbeard at Ocracoke | Blackbeard killed; his head hung from Maynard's bowsprit |
| Oct 1720 | Pirate hunter Jonathan Barnet captures Rackham's sloop off Jamaica | Rackham hanged; Bonny and Read convicted |
| Feb 1722 | Capt. Chaloner Ogle in HMS *Swallow* catches Bartholomew Roberts off Cape Lopez | Roberts killed; mass trial at Cape Coast Castle, 52 hanged |
| July 1726 | William Fly's crew overpowered by prisoners | Fly hanged in Boston |

**5. Mass hangings as propaganda.** Executions were public, sermons were preached at the gallows, and trial records were printed and sold. Bodies were hung in chains at harbour mouths as warnings. Rediker estimates at least 400, and possibly 600, pirates were hanged between 1716 and 1726. As the risk rose, fewer sailors volunteered, and crews could not replace their losses.

**Why it worked when earlier efforts failed.** The colonies had changed. Plantation and slave-trading interests now wanted safe seas more than cheap stolen goods; Britain was at peace and had warships free; and the pirates had no Nassau left to run to.

**Spanish "coast guards".** Piracy did not vanish entirely. In the 1720s and 1730s, Spanish *guardacostas*, licensed privateers based in Cuba and Puerto Rico, seized British smugglers and often plain merchant ships. One victim, Captain Robert Jenkins, claimed a Spanish officer cut off his ear in 1731. The resulting outrage helped start the War of Jenkins' Ear in 1739, and wartime privateering returned with it.

## The last wave (1810s–1830s)

Caribbean piracy had one final surge after the Napoleonic Wars, when Spain's American empire broke apart. It was crushed by the US Navy and Royal Navy by the late 1820s, and privateering itself was abolished in 1856.

**Cause.** From about 1810, Spain's colonies fought for independence. The new rebel governments (Venezuela, Gran Colombia, Mexico, Buenos Aires and short-lived republics like Cartagena) had no navies, so they handed out letters of marque against Spanish shipping. Many holders attacked anyone. At the same time, the end of the Napoleonic Wars in 1815 again left thousands of sailors unemployed.

**Bases and figures:**

- **Jean and Pierre Lafitte** ran a smuggling and privateering base at Barataria Bay near New Orleans. Jean helped the Americans at the Battle of New Orleans in 1815, then moved to Galveston (1817–1821) until the US Navy forced him out.
- **Louis-Michel Aury** held Amelia Island in Spanish Florida in 1817 under rebel commissions.
- **Cuban coast pirates** worked from small boats in the reefs and inlets of northern Cuba. They were far more murderous than the Golden Age crews, often killing whole crews to leave no witnesses, and were quietly protected by local officials and merchants.
- **Roberto Cofresí** of Puerto Rico raided shipping in the early 1820s. He became a folk hero in Puerto Rico.

**Suppression.** The US formed a permanent West India Squadron in 1822. Commodore David Porter took command in 1823 with a "Mosquito Fleet" of eight shallow-draft schooners and rowing barges able to chase pirates into the shallows, based at Key West. The Royal Navy ran parallel patrols, and Spain was pressured into cooperating. In 1824 Porter landed troops at Fajardo, Puerto Rico, without orders and was court-martialled. Cofresí was captured in early 1825 and shot in San Juan that March, often called the last of the West India pirates. Within a few years organised Caribbean piracy was over.

**The end of privateering.** The Declaration of Paris (1856) abolished privateering among the major European powers. The legal grey zone that had fed Caribbean piracy for three centuries was finally closed.

## Myth vs reality

Most of the modern pirate image comes from two books: Johnson's *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724), part fact and part invention, and Robert Louis Stevenson's novel *Treasure Island* (1883). Hollywood did the rest.

| Myth | Reality |
| --- | --- |
| Pirates buried treasure and drew maps | Almost never. Loot was shared and spent fast. William Kidd buried some on Gardiners Island, New York, in 1699; it was dug up as evidence. Treasure maps come from *Treasure Island* |
| Walking the plank | Very rare; a handful of reports, mostly from the 1780s onward. Prisoners were more often marooned, beaten or simply released |
| One Jolly Roger | Each captain had his own flag. Black flags with skulls, skeletons, hourglasses and weapons were common from about 1718; red meant no quarter |
| Pirates were mindless savages | Crews were organised, voted, and worked by written rules. Cruelty was real but was often a deliberate tool to make victims surrender quickly |
| Ships fought huge cannon battles | Most victims gave up without a fight; pirates wanted the ship and cargo undamaged |
| "Arr" and the pirate accent | Mostly from Robert Newton's West Country accent as Long John Silver in the 1950 *Treasure Island* film. Real pirates spoke ordinary sailors' English of many regions |
| Eye patches, peg legs and parrots | Injuries were common, so some had them, but no evidence they were typical. Parrots were sometimes kept or sold as exotic goods |
| Pirates were rich | Most died poor, at sea, of disease, or on the gallows. Careers usually lasted a year or two |
| Pirates were all white men | Crews were mixed; Black sailors were common, and a few women (Bonny, Read) served |

The more radical modern reading, from historians like Marcus Rediker, sees pirate ships as a working-class revolt against brutal merchant and navy discipline. Economists like Peter Leeson read the same evidence as self-interested criminals finding rules that paid. Both agree pirates were far more organised than the legend suggests.

## Notes for the pirate game

The real history already contains most of the systems planned for the Godot piracy and trade sim. These are the historical hooks for each.

| Game system | Historical basis | Mechanic ideas |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Privateer vs pirate paths | Letters of marque made raiding legal against named enemies only; peace treaties suddenly made the same acts piracy (1670, 1697, 1713) | A licence that limits legal targets; prize court takes a cut; a treaty event that voids licences overnight and pushes privateers towards piracy |
| Contracts | Governors commissioned raids (Modyford and Morgan); colonies hired pirate hunters (Rhett, Maynard, Barnet) | Raid, escort, smuggling and pirate-hunting contracts from rival governors |
| Reputation and pardons | The 1717 pardon; Blackbeard and Hornigold taking it; Rogers hiring ex-pirates | A pardon event with a deadline; separate reputation per nation; option to go legit and hunt old friends |
| Bases | Tortuga, Port Royal, Petit-Goâve, Nassau: shallow harbours, fresh water, near shipping lanes, weak government | Base value from location, defences, market access and governor attitude; bases can be retaken by a navy (Nassau 1718) or lost to disaster (Port Royal 1692) |
| Crew with stats and traits | Specialists (surgeon, carpenter, gunner, navigator) were scarce and often forced; crews were mixed nationalities and backgrounds | Forced vs volunteer crew affects loyalty; specialists give large bonuses; ex-slaves, ex-navy, ex-merchant backgrounds |
| Mutiny and elections | Captains elected and voted out (Vane 1718); the quartermaster checked the captain; Roberts elected after Davis died | Crew approval meter driven by loot, food, risk and fairness; quartermaster as a second power centre; on captain death, a vote between candidates with their own traits |
| Articles | Written codes with shares, rules and injury pay | Player-written articles: more shares for the captain cost loyalty; injury pay costs money but improves recruitment |
| Combat | Most prizes surrendered to fear; boarding over broadsides | A morale-based surrender check before combat; black vs red flag choice; reputation for cruelty makes surrender more likely but brings navy attention |
| Market | Pirate goods sold at a discount through fences; Navigation Acts made colonists keen buyers; coin was scarce | Legal ports vs fences with different prices and risk; heat level per port; governors who can be bribed (Eden) |
| Political intrigue | Shifting wars between Spain, Britain, France and the Netherlands; Spain's *guardacostas*; slave-trade lobbies pushing for suppression | Periodic war and peace states that change which targets are legal; a rising anti-piracy pressure meter that brings warships |
| Endgame pressure | Royal Navy patrols, new laws and hangings ended the era in about ten years | A ticking clock: piracy gets harder over time, pushing the player to retire, take a pardon, or go out in a blaze |

Timeline options: setting the game around 1715–1720 gives the 1715 wreck, Nassau, the pardon and the navy crackdown all in one window, which suits a deep, narrow game.

Books worth reading:

- Alexandre Exquemelin, *The Buccaneers of America* (1678). First-hand buccaneer account.
- Captain Charles Johnson, *A General History of the Pyrates* (1724). The original pirate biographies; part invented.
- David Cordingly, *Under the Black Flag* (1995). The standard readable history, strong on myth vs fact.
- Marcus Rediker, *Villains of All Nations* (2004). Pirate social life and politics.
- Colin Woodard, *The Republic of Pirates* (2007). Narrative history of Nassau and the Flying Gang.
- Peter Leeson, *The Invisible Hook* (2009). The economics of pirate organisation.
- Kris Lane, *Pillaging the Empire: Global Piracy on the High Seas, 1500–1750* (1998). The wider context.

Some details (for example the 1523 Fleury capture, the Piet Heyn haul, and Royal Navy manpower after 1713) come from standard histories rather than the pages above.
